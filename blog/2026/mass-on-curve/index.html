For my first technical post, I’ll share a classical mechanics problem that I thought of while not fully paying attention during a lecture a while ago. I caught the physics bug while taking AP Physics 1 (classical mechanics) in junior year of high school, so I thought this would be an appropriate way to start.

A point mass $m$ travels along the smooth function $y(x): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ under gravity and friction. Find the equation of motion for this particle.

I'm imagining a point mass using this curve $y(x)$ as a slide. For instance, take $y(x) = \sin(x) / x$; the figure below shows the particle placed at the point $(a, \sin(a)/a)$, ready to be released so we can observe its subsequent motion. Our goal is to find the governing differential equation of this motion. We will account for both the static and kinetic coefficients of friction. We ignore air resistance.

The setup: a point mass placed at rest at $(a, \sin(a)/a)$ on the slide $y(x) = \sin(x)/x$ (here $a = 1.5$), ready to be released.

Equation of motion derivation: frictionless case

To solve this problem, we turn to Lagrangian mechanicsIncidentally, for a long time, I was led to believe that Lagrangian mechanics cannot be applied to systems with nonconservative forces. That is certainly false, as we will see shortly. Incidentally, on a deeper level it is also true that there is no such thing as a nonconservative force. See here: https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_14.html.. This might be opening a can of worms if you have not seen Lagrangian mechanics before. For a good introduction to this topic, see this Feynman lecture. Please note that in this lecture, Feynman does not mention the dissipation function, which we will need here due to the frictional force. Also, I found an extremely helpful forum post which has the answer to my question. However, I feel that this person’s response could be made clearer. There is no mention of the dissipation function or radius of curvature. Even if I do not go into depth on certain topics, I think it is helpful to at least mention them explicitly so that someone who is interested has specific search terms that they can use to look into things further. Apart from that, there is a sign error in an intermediate step (on the potential energy) and there is a simplification that can be made for a certain term involving the signum function. That being said, I want to reiterate that this person’s answer was very useful and I was able to use it to check my own final answer.

To make the solution method a little more digestible, let’s begin with the frictionless case. In this case, we only need to take note of the functional known as the “action,” defined by \begin{equation} S[q(t)] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} L(q(t), \dot{q}(t),t)~\mathrm{d}t, \end{equation} where $q(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$Here I'm assuming $q(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ because we are only concerned with a single particle's $x$-coordinate (i.e., $q(t) = x(t)$) but we could certainly also have the vectorized version $\vec{q}(t): \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}^n$ to keep track of several particles in multiple dimensions. describes the motion of the particle in generalized coordinates These are the specific set of coordinates used for the problem at hand. We will be using Cartesian coordinates but for a different problem (e.g., those involving a pendulum) polar coordinates may be more appropriate, for example. as a function of time $t$, $L$ is the Lagrangian, and we have used Newton’s dot notation to denote differentiation with respect to time. Further, we are assuming our observation takes place over the time interval $[t_0, t_1]$.

Why do we care about this action thing? Well, whatever mechanism that created the universe that we live in decided that between the points $x(t_0) = x_0$ and $x(t_1) = x_1$, the particle will follow the trajectory $x(t)$ for which the action $S$ is stationary, i.e., $\delta S = 0$. Despite having known this for about 8 years now, I still find this principle a bit magical. The principle of least action is equivalent to Newton’s second law, but then you might say it is also not obvious why the second law should be in the form that it is. (Why is it so simple? Why is it a second order differential equation? Why not third order? Fourth? It could have been an integro-differential equation. It could have been nonlinear. The possibilities are endless.) Again, it appears that these are simply the rules of the game that have been laid out, and what’s left for us to do is to play by them, however magical they may seem. We don’t ask why the bishop moves diagonally or why the knight jumps in the shape of an “L”; we play chess!

In classical mechanics, the Lagrangian takes a simple formTo stress the point, let me clearly state that this Lagrangian is specifically for classical mechanics. If we were dealing with electromagnetism for example, the Lagrangian would be something else (albeit similar).: it is the kinetic energy of the particle minus its potential energy. Plugging this in, we have \begin{equation} S[x(t)] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1} \frac{1}{2} m |\vec{v}|^2 - mgy ~\mathrm{d}t\end{equation} where we have switched from $q(t)$ to $x(t)$ to show that the generalized coordinate we’re using is the position along the $x$-axis in Cartesian coordinates, $\vec{v} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ is the particle velocityTherefore $|\vec{v}|$ is speed., $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity at sea level, and $y$ is the height of the particle as defined in the problem statement. In the interest of cleaner notation, we have suppressed the dependence of $v$ and $y$ on $x(t)$, but this dependence should be obvious from context. From this post onward, you can safely assume we are working with metric units unless I lose my mind and switch to something else, in which case I will point it out. Knowing that we’re using metric units, you can see that the Lagrangian has units of joules and that the action has units of joule-seconds. To minimize the action, we use variational calculus. I can make a post on that if requested.

Variational calculus tells us that the minimizing $x(t)$ should satisfy the Euler-Lagrange equation, \begin{equation} \label{EL} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = 0. \end{equation} As stated, the Lagrangian for our problem is simply the kinetic energy minus the potential energy. The kinetic energy is \begin{align} \frac{1}{2} m |\vec{v}|^2 &= \frac{1}{2} m (v_x^2 + v_y^2), \end{align} where $v_x$ is the velocity in the $x$-direction and $v_y$ is the velocity in the $y$-direction. Since $x(t)$ is the $x$-coordinate of the position of the particle, $v_x$ is simply $\dot{x}(t)$. Similarly, $v_y$ is $\dot{y}(t)$, which we can write in terms of $\dot{x}(t)$ by using the chain rule, yielding $\dot{y}(t) = \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\dot{x}(t)$. Therefore, \begin{align} \vec{v} &= \dot{x}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \newline \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \end{bmatrix} \newline \implies |\vec{v}|^2 &= \dot{x}^2\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right] \end{align}

Meanwhile, the potential energy is $mgy$. Therefore, the Lagrangian is \begin{equation} \label{lagrangian} L = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right] - mgy.\end{equation} Now we compute the different terms in the Euler-Lagrange equation: \begin{align} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} &= m \dot{x}\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2 \right] \newline \implies \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} &= m \ddot{x}\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2 \right] + 2m\dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}\end{align} and \begin{equation} \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = m \dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} - mg \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}. \end{equation} Plugging into \eqref{EL} and rearranging, we have

\begin{equation} \ddot{x} = - \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \frac{g + \dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}}{1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}. \label{frictionless} \end{equation}

Awesome! We now have a compact description of how a particle would traverse the frictionless curve $y(x)$. This is an autonomous,There is no explicit $t$ dependence. explicit,The highest order term $\ddot{x}$ is isolated on one side and written as a function of the independent variable, the dependent variable, and lower-order derivatives. nonlinear,It contains $\dot{x}^2$. second-orderThe highest number of derivatives we take of the unknown function $x(t)$ is two. ordinaryThere is only one independent variable $t$. differential equation. You might say that the position of the particle as a function of time is described by $x(t)$ (and $y(x(t))$) which is not what we have here. That may be true, but we can solve \eqref{frictionless} numerically for $x(t)$. I used the SciPy (a Python library) ODE integrator called $\texttt{solve\_ivp}$ to create the figures for this post. See the figure below for a point particle traversing a frictionless parabola.

A point mass sliding on the frictionless parabola $y = x^2$, released from rest at $x = -2$. With no energy lost, it oscillates forever.

Only for a very simple curve $y(x)$ could we ever hope to get a closed-form solution for $x(t)$. For $y(x) = x$, $\dot{x}(0) = 0$, $x(0) = 0$, we obtain $x(t) = -gt^2/4$ (and therefore $y(t) = -gt^2/4$). Let’s do a couple of sanity checks. For one, these expressions have the correct units of meters, since $g$ has units of meters per seconds squared and $t^2$ has units of seconds squared, giving meters once multiplied together. Also, as $t$ increases, notice that $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ become increasingly negative. That’s exactly what we should expect if we place the particle at the origin on the curve $y(x) = x$. It should slide down and to the left, which is exactly what our expressions for $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are telling us. The velocity of the particle is also becoming increasingly negative in both components which is also to be expected since the particle is under the influence of gravity. One interesting feature of \eqref{frictionless} is that it is independent of the particle’s mass.

Adding friction

Now let’s return to the original problem, which involves friction. To handle that, we need to modify \eqref{EL}. Allow me to pull a couple more fast ones on you (the first one was the concept of “action”). First, we need to insert a “generalized force” on the right-hand side and second, we will need to find a coefficient of this generalized force. A generalized force is one that is defined over generalized coordinates. We have already specified that we will be working with the generalized coordinate $x(t)$ here and so the generalized force we will add is in terms of this. It might feel pedantic that I’m calling it a “generalized force” and not just a “force” but I want to use the proper terms so that you will have some familiarity with them already if you decide to read further and come across them. Otherwise, they might seem rather alien and you might feel cheated by my current usage of Lagrangian mechanics if you saw those terms in the wild but not here.

As stated, let’s add a generalized force on the right-hand side of \eqref{EL} \begin{equation} \label{modified} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = Q, \end{equation} where \begin{equation} Q = -\frac{\partial D}{\partial \dot{x}},~ D = \frac{1}{N_v+1}\sum_{k} c_k |\vec{v_k}|^{N_v+1} \end{equation} are the generalized force and dissipation function, respectively. This is the modification that will allow us to handle dissipative forces in the Lagrangian formalism. The sum is over the $k$ objects in a system so for this problem, we will only retain one term. Further, $N_v$ is the order of the velocity dependence for the friction we are considering. In our case, we are not considering velocity-dependent friction the way we would have to if we were considering air resistance, for example. Therefore, $N_v=0$. We already found $\vec{v}$ (I’m suppressing the subscript because we only have one object) earlier and used it in \eqref{lagrangian}. Using that same $\vec{v}$, we find $D = c|\dot{x}|\sqrt{1 + (\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x})^2}$. Plugging this into \eqref{modified}, we have \begin{equation} \label{modified2} \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x} = -c\mathrm{sgn}(\dot{x})\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}\end{equation} for $\dot{x} \neq 0$. The $\dot{x} = 0$ case requires us to consider static friction, which we will discuss shortly.

Let’s do some dimensional analysis. On the left-hand side of \eqref{modified2}, we have the term $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}$. As stated earlier, the Lagrangian has units of joules. Therefore, this term has units of joules per meter, or newtons. That implies the right-hand side must also have units of newtons and they must be contained in the $c$ term, since $\sqrt{1 + (\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x})^2}$ is unitless. This is the third and final fast one I will pull: $c$ is the force of friction. Of course, friction had to show up somewhere and $c$ has units of force so it feels morally correct to put them together but it is reasonable to question why $c$ is exactly equal to friction. It could have been some dimensionless constant times the force of friction, where that constant is not equal to one. Yet, that is not the case. At this point, I have to confess my ignorance. Please enlighten me if you have an explanation for this. Allow me to set $c = F_{\mu_k}$ to denote this relationship and move onward.

Friction is proportional to the normal force, where the proportionality constant is the friction coefficient, i.e., $F_{\mu_k} = \mu_k N$. So now the task is to determine $N$. To do this, we need to recall the topic of uniform circular motion: when an object is constrained to move on a circular path at constant speed. Let’s pretend for a moment that our particle is undergoing uniform circular motion. Then, a free body diagram would reveal the relationship \begin{equation} \label{normal} N - (mg)_{\perp} = \frac{m|\vec{v}|^2}{r}, \end{equation} where $(mg)_{\perp}$ is the component of gravity perpendicular to the circular path and pointing away from the center of the circle and $r$ is the radius. Some simple trigonometry reveals that $(mg)_{\perp} = mg\cos(\theta)$, where $\theta$ is the angle the particle makes with the horizontal. I again apologize for the lack of illustration here but I’m hoping that you might recall or be able to quickly rederive this trigonometric fact. It is exactly the same angle $\theta$ you would find in a standard inclined plane problem. Another trigonometric fact we need is that $\tan(\theta)$ is exactly equal to $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$. This holds for any reasonable $y(x)$, not just a circular arc. Therefore, after writing $\cos(\theta)$ in terms of $\tan(\theta)$, we have \begin{equation} \cos(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}}. \end{equation} That takes care of $(mg)_{\perp}$, and we would have everything we need to find the normal force if the particle was moving along a circular trajectory with radius $r$. However, we want an expression for the normal force (and therefore friction) to hold for a more general $y(x)$, not just one that traces out a circular arc. For a more general $y(x)$, we can treat the curve locally as part of a circular arc. In other words, if we zoom in enough, the particle appears to be traversing along a circle and from one point to the next along $y(x)$, it is traversing along circles of different radii. As an extreme example, even if the particle were traveling in a straight line, we could still treat it as traversing on a circular arc; one with radius equal to infinity. Hopefully, this makes some sense intuitively but is there a way to properly quantify the radius associated with each point of $y(x)$? The answer is yes (or else we would be in trouble and I would have to end this post here)! Borrowing from differential geometry, the signed radius of curvature we want is \begin{equation} r = \frac{(1 + (\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x})^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}{\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}}. \end{equation} And with that, we have everything we need to find the normal force. Rearranging \eqref{normal} and plugging in what we just found, we have \begin{align} N = m\frac{g + \dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}}{\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}}.\end{align}

We now have everything we need to plug into \eqref{modified2}, which yields \begin{align} m\ddot{x}\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right] + m\dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} + mg\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} &= -\mu_k N \mathrm{sgn}(\dot{x})\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2} \newline &= -\mu_k m \mathrm{sgn}(\dot{x})\left(g + \dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}\right).\end{align} After rearranging, we have

\begin{equation} \ddot{x} = -\frac{ \left(g + \dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}\right) \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} + \mu_k\,\mathrm{sgn}(\dot{x})\right) }{ 1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2 }. \label{frictionEOM} \end{equation}

Nice! We now have a compact description of the particle motion for the more difficult case where we take friction into account. If we plug in $\mu_k = 0$, we get back to the frictionless equation of motion given by \eqref{frictionless}, as we should. As in the frictionless case, \eqref{frictionEOM} is also independent of the particle’s mass.

Energy dissipation

It is also useful to check what friction is doing to the particle’s mechanical energy. The total mechanical energy is \begin{equation} E = \frac{1}{2}m\dot{x}^2\left[1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right] + mgy. \end{equation} Taking the time derivative and using the chain rule gives \begin{align} \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} &= m\dot{x}\ddot{x}\left[1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right] +m\dot{x}^3\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} +mg\dot{x}\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \newline &= m\dot{x}\left[ \ddot{x}\left(1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right) +\dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} +g\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} \right]. \end{align} Substituting \eqref{frictionEOM} into this expression, the terms arising from gravity and the curvature of the track cancel, leaving \begin{equation} \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = -m\mu_k|\dot{x}| \left( g+\dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} \right). \end{equation} Recall that we found the normal force to be \begin{equation} N = m\frac{g+\dot{x}^2\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}} {\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}}, \end{equation} while the speed of the particle is \begin{equation} |\vec{v}| = |\dot{x}|\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}. \end{equation} Therefore,

\begin{equation} \frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}t} = -\mu_k N|\vec{v}| \leq 0. \end{equation}

In other words, kinetic friction monotonically removes mechanical energy from the particle. This gives us an energetic explanation for the decaying oscillations we will see in the numerical solutions below.

What happens at rest?

Back at the start I promised we would account for static friction, so let’s make good on that. The key realization is that the equation of motion \eqref{frictionEOM} only describes the particle while it is moving. But what about the instants when the particle is momentarily at rest, like the top of a swing or the very moment we release it? That is the domain of static friction, and notice that it appears nowhere in \eqref{frictionEOM}.

Kinetic friction has a fixed magnitude $\mu_k N$ and always points against the motion. Static friction is instead a reaction force: it supplies exactly as much force as needed to prevent sliding, up to a ceiling of $\mu_s N$.

So static friction poses exactly one new question: at a moment of rest, does the particle stay put or begin to slide? We answer it with a force balance. At rest there is no centripetal term, so the normal force is just $N = mg\cos(\theta)$. Gravity’s pull along the track is $mg\sin(\theta)$, directed downhill. To keep the particle still, static friction must cancel that pull, which it can do only up to its ceiling: \begin{equation} mg\sin(\theta) \le \mu_s\, mg\cos(\theta) \implies \tan(\theta) \le \mu_s. \end{equation} Since we already found that $\tan(\theta) = \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}$, the condition to stay stuck is wonderfully simple: \begin{equation} \left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\rvert \le \mu_s. \end{equation} The particle sticks wherever the slope is shallow enough. This is the classic angle of friction: the steepest angle $\theta_{\max} = \arctan(\mu_s)$ at which static friction can still hold. Wherever $\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\rvert > \mu_s$, the particle must slide, and \eqref{frictionEOM} takes over.

The points where $\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\rvert \le \mu_s$ form resting bands along the curve, and these bands are the only places the particle can ever come to rest. The reason is simple: if the particle reaches zero velocity while inside a band, static friction is strong enough to hold it and it stays put for good; if it stops anywhere else, the slope beats static friction and it immediately slides on.With $\mu_s = 0$ these bands collapse to just the exact stationary points ($\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = 0$), and the particle would creep infinitely slowly toward the very bottom, never quite settling in finite time. Turning $\mu_s$ on is exactly what makes the "it comes to rest" story clean. For the figures below I take $\mu_s = \mu_k$, a common simplification. If we set the particle down at rest inside a band, it never moves.

To make the resting bands concrete, here they are drawn on the $\sin(x)/x$ slide. The shaded strips mark every stretch shallow enough that static friction could hold the particle at rest. They widen around the flat troughs and crests and pinch to nothing on the steep descents.

The resting bands on $y = \sin(x)/x$ (here $\mu_s = 0.1$), shaded where $\left\lvert\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right\rvert \le \mu_s$. The particle can only come to rest on the bolded stretches; everywhere else the slope is too steep and it must keep sliding.

Leaving the track

So far we’ve quietly assumed the particle stays glued to the curve. But a slide can only push—it can’t pull the particle down onto itself. So the normal force must satisfy $N \ge 0$. Recall that we found \begin{equation} N = m\,\frac{g + \dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}}{\sqrt{1+\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2}}. \end{equation} The mass and the denominator are positive, so the sign of $N$ is just the sign of $g + \dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2}$. On a concave-up stretch ($\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} > 0$, the inside of a valley) that is always positive and all is well. But on a concave-down stretch ($\frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} < 0$, the crest of a hill), a fast-enough particle demands more centripetal force than gravity can provide; $N$ would have to turn negative to keep it down, and since the track cannot pull, the particle leaves the surface and sails off as a projectile. The condition to stay on the track is simply \begin{equation} g + \dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} \ge 0. \end{equation}

This is the same reason a roller coaster throws you out of your seat over a hill taken too fast, and it becomes a genuine constraint once we go hunting for more interesting curves. Consider a double-well quartic, $y = 0.1x^4 - 0.4x^2$: two valleys with a hill between them. Released from one side with carefully chosen coefficients, the particle rolls over the central hill and settles in the far valley, staying on the track the whole way. Make the hill steeper or drop it from higher, and it is possible that it would fly off at the hill instead.I checked numerically that the particle shown keeps $g + \dot{x}^2 \frac{\mathrm{d}^2y}{\mathrm{d}x^2} \ge 0$ for its entire trip, so it never actually leaves the track.

Phase space

Let’s take another way of seeing the same motion. Instead of watching the particle on the curve, we plot its state in the phase plane: position $x$ on one axis, momentum on the other. Which momentum? The natural choice is one we have already met, the canonical momentum conjugate to $x$, which is exactly the quantity $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}}$ we computed on the way to the Euler-Lagrange equation, \begin{equation} p = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}} = m\dot{x}\left[1 + \left(\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x}\right)^2\right]. \end{equation}

A single point $(x, p)$ captures the entire state of the particle, and as time runs that point traces out a curve. Without friction, energy is conserved, so the particle is pinned to a constant energy contour and its phase-space curve is a closed loop, retracing the same orbit forever. With friction, energy leaks away, the particle can no longer close its loop, and instead it spirals inward: each loop is smaller than the one before, winding in until the motion comes to rest. Energy loss, which we found leaks away at rate $\mu_k N\lvert\vec{v}\rvert$, becomes visible as the steady shrinking of the orbit’s loops.Recall that the trajectory itself does not depend on the mass, so for the figure I simply set $m = 1$.

The figure below shows this for the double-well quartic. The gray loop is the frictionless orbit the particle would trace forever; the colored curve (shaded by time) is the real, frictional trajectory, peeling off that loop and spiraling in to rest inside the right-hand side well.

Under the given conditions, the particle does not oscillate in place; it makes one long rightward run, turns around a single time, and settles in a distant trough.

Where to go from here

There are several extensions we can make to this problem.

  • An ant with a jetpack. Suppose that the particle is a tiny intelligent ant named Flik, who has his own jetpack. It provides him thrust tangential to the curve at any given point. Flik is an adventurer; he wants to reach as large an $x$-coordinate as possible. Suppose Flik wants to stay on the curve during his journey and that his jetpack has a finite fuel supply. Find the optimal policy on how to use the jetpack. This is an optimal control problem, and it would be interesting to see whether the optimal policy can be derived analytically. Alternatively, we could take a learning-based approach, such as reinforcement learning, and have Flik learn a strategy through trial and error.
  • Air resistance / velocity-dependent friction. We could include velocity-dependent air resistance and in that case we would require a dissipation function with $N_v \ne 0$.
  • Rolling instead of sliding. Here we assumed a point particle, which necessarily can only slide along the given function. We could instead consider an extended particle, which would have a moment of inertia and could roll along the curve.
  • From a curve to a surface $z(x,y)$. We could extend our one-dimensional study here to two dimensions.
  • Families of curves. We could try to classify families of curves for given initial conditions that allow the particle to reach a certain $x$-coordinate. It is obvious that if we were to release the particle from rest at a point where $\frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} > 0$, it would never reach an $x$-coordinate to the right of that point. Then what are the conditions on $y(x)$ that do allow the particle to reach a prescribed destination?
